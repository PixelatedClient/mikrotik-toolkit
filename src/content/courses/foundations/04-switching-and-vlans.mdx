## What a switch does

A switch forwards **Ethernet frames** inside one network using **MAC addresses**. A router connects different networks; a switch builds one.

A switch learns as it goes:

1. A frame arrives on port 3 from MAC `aa:aa`. The switch records *aa:aa is on port 3*.
2. If the destination MAC is in the table, the frame goes out only that port.
3. If it is unknown, or a broadcast, the frame is **flooded** out every other port.

The MAC table entries expire after a few minutes of silence.

## Broadcast domains

Every port on a switch belongs to one **broadcast domain**: a broadcast from one device reaches all the others. In a big flat network, broadcasts (ARP, DHCP) become noise, and every device can see every other. That is bad for performance and security.

## VLANs

A **VLAN** (Virtual LAN) splits one physical switch into several separate broadcast domains. Devices in VLAN 10 cannot reach VLAN 20 at Layer 2. To talk across VLANs, traffic must go through a **router** (inter-VLAN routing), where you can apply a firewall.

Typical split:

| VLAN | Purpose | Subnet |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | Staff | 10.10.10.0/24 |
| 20 | Guests | 10.10.20.0/24 |
| 99 | Management | 10.10.99.0/24 |

One VLAN, one subnet. Keep that rule and troubleshooting stays easy.

## Access and trunk ports

- **Access port**: belongs to one VLAN. Frames are untagged. Laptops, printers and phones connect here.
- **Trunk port**: carries several VLANs between switches or to a router. Each frame carries an **802.1Q tag** with a 12-bit VLAN ID field — MikroTik's own VLAN documentation confirms the valid range as 1-4094 (4096 possible 12-bit values, with 0 and 4095 reserved).

## Loops and spanning tree

Ethernet frames have no hop limit. If two switches are connected by two cables, a broadcast circles forever and takes the network down within seconds: a **broadcast storm**.

**Spanning Tree Protocol** (STP, RSTP) elects a root switch and blocks redundant links so the topology becomes a loop-free tree. If an active link fails, a blocked one takes over. Classic 802.1D STP relies on fixed timers — Cisco's own documentation puts a typical convergence delay at about 30 seconds (twice the default 15-second forward delay). **RSTP** (802.1w) instead converges "sometimes in the order of a few hundred milliseconds," without depending on those timers at all — use RSTP rather than legacy STP wherever the switches support it.
