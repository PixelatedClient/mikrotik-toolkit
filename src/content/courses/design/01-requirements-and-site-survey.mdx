## Design starts with questions, not routers

A design is a promise: "this network will do X for Y users at Z availability." You can only keep that promise if you write X, Y and Z down before you buy anything. Most bad networks were not built badly. They were built for the wrong requirements.

Ask, in this order:

1. **Who uses it?** Count users and devices today, and guess the count in three years. Phones, cameras and printers count as devices.
2. **What do they run?** Web and email are light. Video calls, backups, cameras and VoIP need bandwidth or low delay, and some need to stay up even when the internet does not.
3. **How much downtime is acceptable?** A shop that closes at night can survive a night repair. A clinic cannot. Availability targets drive how much redundancy you pay for.
4. **What must be kept apart?** Guests from staff, cameras from the office, payment devices from everything.
5. **What is the budget and who will run it?** A network nobody can operate is a bad design, however elegant.

## Turn answers into numbers

| Requirement | Number you design with |
|---|---|
| Users and devices | Hosts per segment, now and in 3 years |
| Applications | Peak bandwidth per segment and to the internet |
| Availability | Allowed downtime per year |
| Separation | Number of segments (VLANs) |
| Growth | Spare addresses and spare ports (aim for 30 to 50 percent) |

A useful rule: **design for the peak, plan for the growth, and write the assumptions down.** When something changes later, you can see which assumption broke.

Availability is easier to feel as time. Allowed downtime per year: 99 percent is about 3.7 days, 99.9 percent about 8.8 hours, 99.99 percent about 53 minutes.

## The site survey

Before drawing anything, walk the site. Note:

- where the internet arrives and where power is available,
- cable routes, distances and what is already installed,
- rooms that will hold equipment, and how they are cooled and locked,
- where wireless is needed and what could block it,
- who owns each decision (landlord, ISP, security).

Take photos. A simple table of rooms, devices and cable runs is worth more than a beautiful diagram made later from memory.

## A worked example

A dental clinic with 3 floors: 25 staff, 8 treatment rooms, a reception, guests waiting, 6 cameras and one X-ray server. It cannot lose patient records and must open every day.

- Segments: staff, clinical devices, guest, cameras, servers, management (6).
- Hosts: staff 60 devices, clinical 20, guest 40, cameras 6, servers 4, management 10. Add growth and round up.
- Availability: two internet links (fibre plus a backup 4G link) and a spare router, because a day of downtime costs real appointments.
- Uptime for the X-ray server: UPS power and a nightly backup that is tested.

You now have a list you can turn into an address plan, VLANs and a redundancy design. The next lessons do exactly that.

## Check yourself

- What is the difference between a requirement and a solution? ("We need two firewalls" is a solution. "We cannot be down for more than an hour" is a requirement.)
- Why write assumptions down?
- What does 99.9 percent availability allow per year?
