## What BGP is for

BGP (Border Gateway Protocol) is the protocol of the internet. It exchanges **reachability between autonomous systems**, not internal topology. Where OSPF asks "what is the shortest path?", BGP asks "what does policy say we should prefer?".

An **autonomous system (AS)** is a network under one administration with a common routing policy. It has an **ASN**. Private ASNs are 64512-65534 (and 4200000000-4294967294 for 32-bit); public ASNs are allocated by the regional registries.

## eBGP and iBGP

- **eBGP**: between different ASNs, usually directly connected. Learned routes get your neighbour's ASN prepended.
- **iBGP**: between routers **inside** your AS to carry external routes across your network. iBGP does not modify the AS path, and a route learned from one iBGP peer is **not** passed to another iBGP peer. That rule forces a full mesh, or route reflectors (see [route reflection](/learn/routing/05-route-reflection-confederations)).

## Session states

BGP runs over TCP port 179 — RFC 4271 states this directly. A session moves through:

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Idle | Not trying yet |
| Connect / Active | Trying TCP; failing here means no reachability, a firewall, or wrong address |
| OpenSent / OpenConfirm | TCP is up, exchanging OPEN messages; failing here means an ASN or parameter mismatch |
| **Established** | Routes are exchanged |

## What BGP carries

Each route (a **prefix**) comes with **path attributes**:

- **AS_PATH**: RFC 4271 defines this as identifying "the autonomous systems through which routing information... has passed" — the ASNs the route crossed. Also loop prevention: a router rejects a route containing its own ASN.
- **NEXT_HOP**: RFC 4271 defines this as the IP address that "SHOULD be used as the next hop" for the route's destinations — where to send the traffic.
- **LOCAL_PREF**: your preference inside the AS. Higher wins. RFC 4271 is explicit that a router "MUST NOT include this attribute in UPDATE messages it sends to external peers" (outside BGP confederations) — it never leaves your AS.
- **MED**: RFC 4271 calls this MULTI_EXIT_DISC, "intended to be used on external (inter-AS) links to discriminate among multiple exit or entry points to the same neighboring AS" — a hint to a neighbour AS about which of your entry points you prefer. Lower wins.
- **COMMUNITY**: tags used to trigger policy (e.g. `64512:100` = "set local-pref 100").
- **ORIGIN**: how the route was first injected (IGP, EGP or incomplete, per RFC 4271).

## Best-path selection (simplified)

When several routes to the same prefix exist, BGP picks one in this order:

1. Highest **weight** (vendor-specific, local only)
2. Highest **LOCAL_PREF**
3. Locally originated
4. **Shortest AS_PATH**
5. Lowest ORIGIN
6. Lowest **MED** (between routes from the same neighbour AS)
7. eBGP over iBGP
8. Lowest IGP cost to the next hop
9. Oldest / lowest router ID as tie-breakers

In practice, operators control traffic with **local-pref** (outbound) and **AS-path prepending / communities** (inbound). This exact order is what this project's own best-path engine implements, and it was checked against seven attribute scenarios on a real RouterOS 7.16 router — all matched, including that MED is genuinely only ever compared within the same neighbour AS.

## Key rules to remember

- BGP does what you tell it. It has no notion of "best" other than your policy, so **filter everything in and out**.
- A prefix must exist in the routing table before you can originate it.
- Announce the most specific prefix you own, but no longer than /24 for IPv4: most networks filter longer.

## Check yourself

- Why does LOCAL_PREF never leave your own AS, and what does that mean for who it can influence?
- Between two routes learned from two different neighbouring ASes, does the one with the lower MED always win? Why or why not?
