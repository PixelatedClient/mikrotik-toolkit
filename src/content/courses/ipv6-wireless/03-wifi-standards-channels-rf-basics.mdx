## The 802.11 alphabet, decoded

Wi-Fi standards are named after their IEEE 802.11 amendment letter, and more recently by a plain generation number:

| Standard | Generation name | Band(s) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 802.11n | Wi-Fi 4 | 2.4 & 5 GHz | introduced MIMO (multiple antennas) |
| 802.11ac | Wi-Fi 5 | 5 GHz only | wider channels, higher throughput |
| 802.11ax | Wi-Fi 6 / 6E | 2.4, 5, (6 GHz for 6E) | OFDMA, better performance with many clients |
| 802.11be | Wi-Fi 7 | 2.4, 5, 6 GHz | multi-link operation, wider channels still |

Newer standards are backward compatible with older clients on the same band, but a mixed network runs at the speed of its slowest connected features unless the AP is configured to isolate them.

## Bands: 2.4 GHz vs 5 GHz vs 6 GHz

- **2.4 GHz**: longest range, best wall penetration, but only 3 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels (1, 6, 11 in most regulatory domains) and heavy competition from other devices (Bluetooth, microwaves, neighbouring networks) — usually the most congested band in a dense area.
- **5 GHz**: shorter range, more channels, much less congestion, higher achievable throughput. The default choice for most client traffic where range allows.
- **6 GHz** (Wi-Fi 6E/7 only): the newest band, wide open with many more channels than 5 GHz, but even shorter range and only reachable by 6E/7-capable clients.

## Channels and channel width

A band is divided into channels; wider channels (40/80/160 MHz instead of 20 MHz) carry more data but leave fewer non-overlapping channels available in the same space, and are more prone to interference in a dense deployment. This is a direct throughput-vs-capacity trade-off: a single AP in an isolated house benefits from the widest channel it can get; an office with a dozen APs needs narrower channels so neighbouring APs don't collide.

## Why overlapping channels matter

Two APs on the same or overlapping channels within range of each other contend for the same airtime — every transmission on one is noise the other has to wait out, even if their SSIDs and clients are completely unrelated. This is why 2.4 GHz deployments stick to 1/6/11: any other channel choice overlaps with a neighbour on one of those.

## RF basics that explain real behaviour

- **Higher frequency = shorter range, better penetration trade-off reversed**: counter to intuition, lower frequencies (2.4 GHz) travel further and through walls better than higher ones (5/6 GHz), because lower frequencies diffract around obstacles more easily.
- **Signal strength (RSSI)** is measured in dBm, a negative number closer to zero being stronger (-50 dBm is a strong signal, -80 dBm is marginal).
- **Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)** matters more than raw signal strength alone: a strong signal in a noisy environment can perform worse than a weaker signal somewhere quiet.
- **Every device on a channel shares airtime.** Wi-Fi is a shared medium like old Ethernet hubs (see the Layer 2 track): more connected clients doesn't just share bandwidth, it multiplies contention overhead.
