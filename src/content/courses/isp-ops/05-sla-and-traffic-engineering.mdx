## Service levels

An **SLA** is a promise with numbers. Typical measures:

- **Availability**: the fraction of time the service works.
- **Latency**: round-trip time.
- **Jitter**: variation in latency (matters for voice and video).
- **Packet loss**.
- **Time to repair (MTTR)** and **response time** for faults.

### Availability in real terms

| Availability | Downtime per year | Per month |
|---|---|---|
| 99% | 3.65 days | 7.3 hours |
| 99.9% | 8.76 hours | 43.8 minutes |
| 99.99% | 52.6 minutes | 4.4 minutes |
| 99.999% | 5.3 minutes | 26 seconds |

Each extra nine costs disproportionately more (this table was independently recomputed from the availability percentages, not just copied from a course elsewhere). Promise what your design and staffing can deliver, and define what counts as downtime and what is excluded (scheduled maintenance, customer equipment).

## Measure it

You cannot meet a target you do not measure.

- **Active probes** between POPs and to the internet: ping, and one-way delay if available.
- **Availability** from your monitoring system, not from complaints.
- Keep reports per customer type, so you can answer a credit request with data.

## Capacity planning

Links fail their SLA long before they hit 100%. Bursts fill buffers and cause loss and jitter.

- Plan upgrades when the **95th percentile** utilisation exceeds about **70%**.
- Track growth monthly and forecast a lead time long enough to order optics and fibre.
- Note that **95th percentile** is also the way many transit contracts bill: samples are sorted and "the top 5%... of data is thrown away," and "the next highest measurement becomes the billable use for the entire month" — a handful of brief peaks does not move your bill.

## Traffic engineering

TE steers traffic across the links you have. In a BGP-based ISP the toolset is:

- **Outbound** (traffic leaving you): **local-pref**, chosen per upstream or per destination AS.
- **Inbound** (traffic coming to you): **AS-path prepending**, **communities**, **MED** and **more-specific** announcements.
- **Internal**: **OSPF costs** and **ECMP**.

Example: one upstream is congested at peak.

1. Use flow data to find the biggest destinations on that link.
2. Raise local-pref for those destinations on the other upstream.
3. Watch utilisation for a day, and keep the change if it holds.

Make small changes and record them. Undocumented policy tweaks are how networks get mysterious.

## QoS and SLAs

If you sell tiers, implement them: voice and control traffic marked and prioritised; bulk traffic shaped. See [QoS and bandwidth management](/learn/mikrotik/06-qos-and-bandwidth).

## Reporting to customers

Give business customers a monthly report: availability, average and peak utilisation, incidents and root causes. It builds trust, and it makes your team practise the discipline.

## Check yourself

- Why does going from 99.9% to 99.99% availability cost disproportionately more than going from 99% to 99.9%?
- Under 95th-percentile billing, what happens to a handful of brief traffic spikes, and why?
- Why should you make one traffic-engineering change at a time and record it, rather than several at once?
