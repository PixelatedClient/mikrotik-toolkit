## Transit versus peering

- **Transit**: you pay an upstream to carry your traffic to the whole internet.
- **Peering**: two networks exchange traffic **directly** for their own customers, usually without payment.

Peering shortens the path (lower latency), reduces transit bills, and makes you less dependent on one provider.

## Internet exchanges

An **IX** is a shared Layer 2 switching fabric where many networks connect to one port and peer with each other.

- **Public peering** across the IX fabric: one port, many sessions.
- **Route server**: the IX runs one that redistributes routes, so you set up one session and receive many peers' routes.
- **Bilateral sessions**: a direct BGP session with a specific network, for more control.
- **Private interconnect (PNI)**: a dedicated cross-connect to a big peer when the volume justifies it.

Typical costs: a port fee (by speed) and a cross-connect or a remote-peering fee.

## When it makes sense

Use your flow data (see [network monitoring](/learn/isp-ops/03-network-monitoring)):

1. List the top destination ASNs by traffic.
2. See which of them are present at an IX you can reach.
3. Estimate the transit cost saved against the port and transport cost.

Content and cloud providers, CDNs and other access networks are the usual big wins.

## Getting ready to peer

- Have your own **ASN** and **address space**.
- Register in **PeeringDB** — described on its own site as "the go-to location for interconnection data," used as "the first stop in making interconnection decisions" — with accurate contact details, traffic levels and policy.
- Publish **IRR** route objects and **RPKI ROAs** so peers can build filters.
- Decide a **peering policy** (open, selective, restrictive) and write it down.

## Configuration principles

On the IX port:

- Use a Layer 3 interface with the IX-assigned address; **no other traffic** on it.
- Enable only the protocols the IX allows. Disable neighbour discovery, proxy ARP and other broadcast-noisy features.
- Filter both directions per neighbour:
  - **Announce**: only your own prefixes and your customers'.
  - **Accept**: bogon-free, no default, no more-specific than /24, with a **prefix limit**.
- Do **not** set a default route towards the IX, and do not let others do it to you: set `next-hop` handling so traffic leaves only for advertised prefixes.
- Apply the **relationship rule**: never announce transit-learned routes to peers.

```
/routing filter rule add chain=ix-out rule="if (bgp-communities any 64512:100) { accept }"
/routing filter rule add chain=ix-out rule="if (dst == 198.51.100.0/24) { accept }"
/routing filter rule add chain=ix-out rule="reject"
```

(See [BGP in ISP networks](/learn/routing/03-bgp-in-isp-networks) for the tagging scheme.)

## Preference

Peers are cheaper than transit, so give them a higher local-pref than upstreams. Make sure a peering failure does not push all traffic onto a transit link that cannot take it: check capacity.

## Operating a peering

- Monitor the session state, prefix counts and port errors.
- Watch **traffic asymmetry** and **port utilisation**; upgrade the port at 70%.
- Handle maintenance notices: IXs announce work in advance.
- Follow MANRS — its filtering, coordination and global-validation actions (see [BGP in ISP networks](/learn/routing/03-bgp-in-isp-networks)) apply directly at an IX port — and respond promptly to abuse and route-leak reports.

## Common problems

- Peer sends **more than the limit**: session shut down until they fix it.
- **Leaked routes** because a peer forgot a filter: your own inbound filters protect you.
- **Wrong next-hop** on route server routes when you have your own policy.
- Asymmetric traffic causing stateful firewall drops: do not put stateful firewalls in the peering path.

## Check yourself

- Why does peering shorten the path and reduce transit bills, compared to sending the same traffic via an upstream?
- What is PeeringDB actually for, and why would a network you want to peer with check it before agreeing?
- Why should you never announce transit-learned routes to a peer?
