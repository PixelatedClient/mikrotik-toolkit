The provisioning workflow, profile-naming advice, the Rx power thresholds and the whole fault guide below are this project's own design guidance and operational experience, not a rule quoted from a standard — the GPON architecture it's built on (the OLT/ONT/ODN model, the wavelengths and TDMA upstream) is the same one sourced in [FTTH & GPON Architecture](/learn/isp-ops/01-ftth-gpon-architecture).

## Vendor note

OLT command lines differ widely between vendors, but the **model** is the same everywhere. This lesson teaches the model and a safe workflow. Translate the steps to your vendor's syntax.

## The provisioning model

Provisioning an ONT normally means:

1. **Register** the ONT on a PON port, by **serial number** (SN) or by a password/LOID.
2. Attach a **profile** describing what the ONT gets: bandwidth, ports, services.
3. Map **service ports**: each service (internet, voice, IPTV) gets a VLAN and a bandwidth template.
4. Configure the ONT itself (via OMCI, or TR-069 for the home gateway features).

### Profiles

Vendors call them line profile, service profile, or bandwidth profile. Think of them as:

- **Line / traffic profile**: T-CONT and GEM ports, upstream/downstream bandwidth (assured and max).
- **Service profile**: which ONT ports exist, their VLAN handling, multicast.

Create **a small number of profiles** and reuse them: `int-100M`, `int-300M`, `int-1G`, `int-voice`. Per-customer custom profiles turn into an unmanageable mess.

### VLAN model

Two common designs:

- **N:1**: one **service VLAN** (S-VLAN) per service per area, shared by many customers. Customers are told apart by port, MAC, or DHCP option 82. Scales well.
- **1:1**: one VLAN per customer (S-VLAN + C-VLAN). Simple isolation, but VLAN numbers run out.

Whichever you pick, document the numbering scheme (for example S-VLAN = `100 + service`, C-VLAN = customer index).

## Auto-find and authorisation

Most OLTs can report **unregistered ONTs** (auto-find). A safe workflow:

1. Install the ONT; it appears as an unregistered SN.
2. Confirm the SN matches the customer order (a typo can activate a neighbour's ONT).
3. Register with the right profile.
4. Verify the light levels and service, then record the SN in your customer database.

## Reading the light

Two numbers matter on every ONT: **Rx power at the ONT** (downstream) and **Rx power at the OLT for that ONT** (upstream).

| Rx power | Meaning |
|---|---|
| -8 to -27 dBm | Healthy range for most Class B+ optics |
| Above -8 dBm | Too strong: check for a missing splitter or attenuator |
| Below -27 dBm | Too weak: dirty connector, bend, bad splice, or too long |

Check your vendor's exact thresholds.

## Fault guide

| Symptom | Likely cause | First check |
|---|---|---|
| ONT offline, **LOS** alarm | Fibre cut, unplugged, or dead ONT | Light at the drop, VFL, patch cord |
| Online but very low Rx | Dirty or damaged connector, tight bend | Clean and re-seat; measure loss |
| ONT flapping | Marginal power, bad connector, failing ONT | Trend the Rx power, swap the ONT |
| **Dying gasp** alarms | Power loss at the customer | Confirm with the customer; not a fibre fault |
| One ONT disturbs the whole PON | **Rogue ONT** transmitting outside its slot | Isolate by disabling ONTs one at a time |
| Registered but no internet | Wrong VLAN or profile, service port missing | Service-port table, upstream VLAN, DHCP |
| Speed lower than the plan | Profile mismatch, port congestion, Wi-Fi limits | Profile, PON utilisation, test wired |
| Many ONTs down at once | Feeder cut or a splitter/OLT card failure | Group the alarms by PON port and card |

## Good habits

- Trend optical power for every ONT weekly, and alert on a drop of more than 3 dB from baseline. It finds degrading fibres **before** the customer calls.
- Group alarms by PON: a pattern across one PON points to the feeder; a single ONT points to the drop.
- Keep a standard test: Rx power, VLAN, DHCP lease, a speedtest, then hand over.
- Never leave an OLT port or ONT with default management credentials.
